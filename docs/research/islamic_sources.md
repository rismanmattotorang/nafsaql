## 1. The Qur'anic vocabulary of the nafs and the "stages" of the soul

**What the Qur'an itself says.** The word *nafs* (pl. *anfus*, *nufus*) occurs very frequently and
most often simply means "self" or "person"; only a handful of verses attach a qualifying adjective
to it, and these became the seeds of the later Sufi "stations" of the soul:

| Term | Verse | Arabic (quran.com) | Rendering (Khattab, as shown on quran.com; cf. Abdel Haleem 2008) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *al-nafs al-ammara bi-l-su'* (the soul that commands to evil) | Q 12:53 | إِنَّ ٱلنَّفۡسَ لَأَمَّارَةُۢ بِٱلسُّوٓءِ | "indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil" (spoken by Joseph, or by the governor's wife, depending on the reading) |
| *al-nafs al-lawwama* (the self-reproaching soul) | Q 75:2 | ٱلنَّفۡسِ ٱللَّوَّامَةِ | "I do swear by the self-reproaching soul" |
| *al-nafs al-mutma'inna* (the tranquil / reassured soul) | Q 89:27 | يَـٰٓأَيَّتُهَا ٱلنَّفۡسُ ٱلۡمُطۡمَئِنَّةُ | "O tranquil soul!" |
| *radiya* / *mardiyya* (well-pleased / well-pleasing) | Q 89:28 | ٱرۡجِعِيٓ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكِ رَاضِيَةٗ مَّرۡضِيَّةٗ | "Return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing" |
| the soul "inspired" with its wickedness and its God-consciousness | Q 91:7-8 | وَنَفۡسٍ وَمَا سَوَّىٰهَا فَأَلۡهَمَهَا فُجُورَهَا وَتَقۡوَىٰهَا | "by the soul and the One who fashioned it, then inspired it with right and wrong" |
| purifying (*zakkaha*) vs. corrupting (*dassaha*) the soul | Q 91:9-10 | قَدۡ أَفۡلَحَ مَن زَكَّىٰهَا وَقَدۡ خَابَ مَن دَسَّىٰهَا | "successful is the one who purifies it, and doomed is the one who corrupts it" |
| restraining the soul from *hawa* (caprice) | Q 79:40 | وَنَهَى ٱلنَّفۡسَ عَنِ ٱلۡهَوَىٰ | "restrained themselves from evil desires" |
| hearts (*qulub*) find rest in remembrance | Q 13:28 | أَلَا بِذِكۡرِ ٱللَّهِ تَطۡمَئِنُّ ٱلۡقُلُوبُ | "in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort" |

**Which labels are Qur'anic and which are later.** Only three *nafs*-phrases occur as fixed
noun-adjective pairs in the text: *ammara bi-l-su'* (12:53), *lawwama* (75:2), *mutma'inna* (89:27).
*Radiya* and *mardiyya* are adjectives applied to the same tranquil soul in 89:28, not names of
separate stages in the Qur'an. "*al-nafs al-mulhama*" (the inspired soul) is a Sufi coinage built
from the verb *alhamaha* in 91:8; the Qur'an never says "*nafs mulhama*". "*al-nafs al-kamila*"
(perfect), "*al-safiyya*" (pure) and "*al-zakiyya*" (purified, from 91:9 *zakkaha*) are likewise
later elaborations. The Encyclopaedia of Islam article "Nafs" (Calverley & Netton) treats the
Qur'anic uses first and then the Sufi and philosophical developments separately; Schimmel 1975
(ch. 3, "The Path") similarly presents the three Qur'anic stages as the core and the longer ladders
as later systematisation.

**The seven-stage ladder.** Later Sufi manuals (widely used in the Khalwati/Jerrahi, Naqshbandi and
Qadiri milieux, and in Ottoman and South-Asian handbooks) arrange the terms as a seven-rung ladder,
most commonly: (1) *ammara*, (2) *lawwama*, (3) *mulhama*, (4) *mutma'inna*, (5) *radiya*,
(6) *mardiyya*, (7) *kamila* (or *safiyya* / *zakiyya*). Orders and manuals differ: some place
*mulhama* after *mutma'inna*; some stop at five; some name the top rung differently; some gloss
*radiya* as "pleasing" and *mardiyya* as "pleased-with" (the reverse of the grammatical sense).
The paper should therefore say "a common seven-stage enumeration" and not present any one ladder as
canonical. The modern Islamic-psychology model of Rothman & Coyle 2018 (grounded-theory interviews
with 18 scholars/practitioners) deliberately adopts only the three Qur'anic stages.

scheme was found on an opened page; the closest items found were a Turkish PhD thesis (M. Usta,
Istanbul 2015) and devotional manuals. Cite Schimmel 1975 and EI2 "Nafs" for the general point.

## 2. Qalb, ruh, 'aql, nafs: al-Ghazali, *Ihya'* Book 21 (*Sharh 'aja'ib al-qalb*)

**The four terms, each with two meanings.** Al-Ghazali opens Book 21 by distinguishing two senses
of each word. *Qalb* is (a) the cone-shaped organ of flesh in the chest and (b) a spiritual
subtlety (*latifa rabbaniyya ruhaniyya*) connected to it which is the knowing, perceiving "real"
human being. *Ruh* is (a) a subtle vapour-like body originating in the physical heart and carried
by the arteries (the physicians' "spirit") and (b) again the same knowing subtlety. *Nafs* is (a) the
aggregate of appetite and anger, the blameworthy "lower self" that the Sufis fight (this is the
sense of 12:53), and (b) the same subtlety, now called *mutma'inna*, *lawwama* or *ammara* according
to its state. *'Aql* is (a) knowledge of the real natures of things, an attribute residing in the
heart, and (b) the perceiving subtlety itself. So for al-Ghazali the four words, in their second
sense, name *one* entity viewed under different aspects (Skellie trans. 2010, ch. 1; Griffel 2009
and the SEP "al-Ghazali" entry stress that he adopts Avicennan faculty-psychology here).

**The armies of the heart (*junud al-qalb*).** The heart has two armies: a visible one (the limbs,
senses) and an invisible one (the faculties). Three inner faculties matter most: *shahwa* (appetite,
the drive that procures what benefits), *ghadab* (anger/irascibility, which repels what harms), and
*'ilm*/*idrak* (knowledge, perception). Al-Ghazali's political image is the heart as a king, *'aql*
as its vizier/counsellor, *shahwa* as a tax-collector/provisioner and *ghadab* as its police or
soldiery: a well-ordered kingdom is one where the king consults the vizier, uses the provisioner and
the police, and does not let either of the latter rule. He also describes four "qualities" mixed in
the human being: predatory (*sab'iyya*, from anger), bestial (*bahimiyya*, from appetite), satanic
(*shaytaniyya*, from the misuse of reason) and lordly (*rabbaniyya*), the last being the proper
source of command (Fons Vitae catalogue description; Skellie 2010).

**The heart as mirror.** Knowledge is the reflection of the forms of things in the mirror of the
heart. Five things prevent the reflection: (1) the mirror is defective (as in a child), (2) it is
tarnished by the "rust" of sins, (3) it is turned away from the object, (4) a veil hangs between
them, (5) the knower does not know in which direction to turn. Polishing is by *dhikr* and by
removing vices; this is the basis of the whole programme of *tazkiyat al-nafs* and of al-Ghazali's
claim that Sufi "unveiling" (*mukashafa*) and the scholars' acquired knowledge differ in method
but not in kind (Skellie 2010; SEP "al-Ghazali", sec. on psychology and inspiration).

**Khawatir and their sources.** A *khatir* is an incoming thought or motion of the heart. Those that
call to good are *ilham* (inspiration), those that call to evil are *waswasa* (whispering). The
cause of *ilham* is an angel (*malak*), the cause of *waswasa* a devil (*shaytan*); the heart's
openness to one is called *tawfiq*, to the other *khidhlan*. Later Sufi manuals (e.g. al-Qushayri)
add a four-fold origin: from God (*khatir rabbani*), from an angel (*malaki*), from the lower self
(*nafsani*), from Satan (*shaytani*). Al-Ghazali notes that the two "lammas" (touches) alternate in
the heart and that *dhikr* repels the satanic one.

**Muraqaba and muhasaba.** These two are the subject of their own book of the *Ihya'*
(Book 38, *Kitab al-muraqaba wa-l-muhasaba*): the servant sets conditions on the self in the
morning, watches it through the day (*muraqaba*, vigilance), and audits it at night (*muhasaba*,
self-accounting), then rebukes, punishes and strives. The vocabulary of "accounting" goes back to
al-Harith al-Muhasibi (d. 243/857), whose very name ("the one who takes account") comes from the
practice (Picken 2011; Knysh 2007, the Risala chapters on *muraqaba* and *muhasaba*). Books 22-23
(*Riyadat al-nafs*, *Kasr al-shahwatayn*; Winter 1995) give the practical programme: character
(*khuluq*) can be changed, the mean between excess and defect is sought, and the two desires of
belly and sex are the first to be broken.

**Hadith on the heart.** The hadith of al-Nu'man b. Bashir ends: "in the body there is a piece of
flesh (*mudgha*); if it is sound the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt the whole body is
corrupt: it is the heart" - Sahih al-Bukhari 52 (Book 2, Hadith 45) and Sahih Muslim 1599a
(Book 22, Hadith 133), both opened on sunnah.com.

**Editions/translations verified.** Skellie (trans.), *Al-Ghazali: The Marvels of the Heart*,
Fons Vitae, 2010, ISBN 978-1-887752-31-2, 152 pp. (Fons Vitae catalogue; Google Books).
Winter (trans.), *Al-Ghazali on Disciplining the Soul and on Breaking the Two Desires*, Islamic

## 3. Ibn Sina (Avicenna): *Kitab al-Nafs* of *al-Shifa'* and the *Najat*

**The three souls.** Following Aristotle, Avicenna defines the soul as the first perfection
(*kamal awwal*) of a natural body possessing organs, and distinguishes the vegetative soul
(nutrition, growth, reproduction), the animal soul (perception and voluntary motion) and the
rational soul (theoretical and practical intellect). The rational soul alone is an immaterial
substance that survives the body (Rahman 1952, the *Najat* chapter; Rahman 1959, the Arabic
*De Anima*; McGinnis 2010 ch. 4-5; SEP "Ibn Sina" by Gutas).

**The five internal senses and their ventricles.** Avicenna systematised the "internal senses"
(*al-hawass al-batina*) and located them in the brain's ventricles: in the front ventricle the
common sense (*al-hiss al-mushtarak*, receives all sensibles) and the retentive imagination
(*al-khayal* / *al-musawwira*, stores sensible forms); in the middle ventricle the estimative
faculty (*al-wahm*, grasps non-sensible "intentions" (*ma'ani*) such as the hostility of the wolf
perceived by the sheep) and the compositive imagination (*al-mutakhayyila*, which combines and
separates stored forms; when used by reason it is called *al-mufakkira*, cogitative); in the rear
ventricle memory (*al-hafiza* / *al-dhakira*, stores intentions). SEP "Arabic and Islamic Psychology
and Philosophy of Mind" (Ivry) and Black 1993 describe the scheme; Black shows that *wahm* also has
a logical role (judgements about particulars, the source of many errors) and is the faculty animals
use for "practical" judgement. Averroes later rejected *wahm* as a separate faculty (Ivry).

**The "flying man".** In *De Anima* I.1 (and again at V.7 and in the *Isharat*) Avicenna asks us to
imagine a person created all at once, suspended in air, limbs apart, with no sensation of anything
external or of his own body: he would still affirm the existence of his self. The point is that
self-awareness is primitive and does not depend on the body, so that the thing affirmed (the soul)
is not identical with the body. Marmura 1986 argues it is a "pointer" (*tanbih*) rather than a
demonstration; Kaukua 2015 gives the fullest modern study of Avicennan self-awareness and its
afterlife (Suhrawardi, Mulla Sadra).

**Stages of intellect.** The theoretical intellect passes through: the material intellect
(*al-'aql al-hayulani*, pure potentiality), the intellect *in habitu* (*bi-l-malaka*, possessing
the primary intelligibles), the actual intellect (*bi-l-fi'l*, able to think secondary intelligibles
at will) and the acquired intellect (*al-'aql al-mustafad*, actually contemplating them). The
intelligibles themselves are received from the separate Active Intellect (*al-'aql al-fa''al*), the
last of the celestial intelligences; "learning" is preparation of the soul for this reception, and
*hads* (intuition) is the capacity to hit the middle term quickly - its perfection is the "sacred
intellect" of the prophet (Gutas 2014, part on the intellect; SEP "Ibn Sina"; Ivry notes that
Avicenna adds *in habitu* to al-Farabi's scheme).

**Practical vs theoretical intellect.** The rational soul has two "faces": the theoretical intellect
(*al-'aql al-nazari*) turned upward to the intelligibles, and the practical intellect
(*al-'aql al-'amali*) turned downward to govern the body and the animal faculties; ethical virtue is
the practical intellect's dominance over appetite and anger (Rahman 1952; McGinnis 2010).

## 4. Ibn 'Arabi

Ibn 'Arabi (d. 638/1240) describes the human being as a microcosm (*al-'alam al-saghir*): "man is
the spirit of the cosmos and the cosmos is the body", and Adam is the all-comprehensive book. The
*Perfect Human* (*al-insan al-kamil*) is the one who realises all the divine names, stands in the
"station of no station" and acts as God's vicegerent (*khalifa*) and the intermediary (*barzakh*)
between God and creation (SEP "Ibn 'Arabi", Chittick; Chittick 1989, esp. the chapters on the
Perfect Man). The heart (*qalb*) is named, he says, from *taqallub*, fluctuation: it is the organ
that follows God's self-disclosures as they change from instant to instant, and so the believer's
heart "encompasses" God in a way that reason (*'aql*, from *'iqal*, the fetter) cannot. The heart
has "two eyes", reason and imagination (*khayal*), and sound knowledge needs both: reason sees
God's incomparability (*tanzih*), imagination His similarity (*tashbih*) (Chittick 1989, ch. on
the heart; SEP). Note that the Sufi saying "neither My heaven nor My earth contain Me, but the heart
of My believing servant contains Me" which Ibn 'Arabi quotes is not found in the canonical hadith
collections; do not cite it as a hadith. The later systematisation of *al-insan al-kamil* is
'Abd al-Karim al-Jili's (d. c. 1408-17) *al-Insan al-kamil*, studied in Nicholson 1921, ch. 2.

## 5. Mulla Sadra and Suhrawardi

**Mulla Sadra** (Sadr al-Din al-Shirazi, d. 1045/1636) builds on the primacy and gradation
(*tashkik*) of existence. *Substantial motion* (*al-haraka al-jawhariyya*) means that change is not
confined to accidents but affects the very existence of substances, which is renewed at every
instant; time is a dimension of this motion. Hence the soul is "bodily in its origination,
spiritual in its subsistence" (*jismaniyyat al-huduth, ruhaniyyat al-baqa'*): it begins as the form
of a body, intensifies through sensory, imaginal and intellectual stages, and ends as an immaterial
intellect that survives the body - against Avicenna, for whom the soul is immaterial from the start
(SEP "Mulla Sadra", Rizvi; Rizvi 2009; Rahman 1975). Knowledge is a mode of existence: in the act of
intellection the intellect and the intelligible are one (*ittihad al-'aqil wa-l-ma'qul*), a thesis
Avicenna rejected and Sadra revived from Porphyry; the most certain knowledge is *knowledge by
presence* (*al-'ilm al-huduri*), the unmediated presence of the known to the knower, as in
self-awareness, which Sadra takes from Suhrawardi but grounds in existence (Kalin 2010; SEP).

**Suhrawardi** (d. 587/1191), founder of the "Illuminationist" (*ishraqi*) school, rejected
Avicenna's representational account and argued that the soul knows itself, its body and its states
by presence (*hudur*), without an intervening form; self-awareness is the paradigm of knowledge
and *dhawq* (taste, intuition) is more reliable than discursive reasoning (SEP "Suhrawardi",
Marcotte; Ha'iri Yazdi 1992, the standard modern monograph on knowledge by presence).

## 6. Al-Muhasibi, al-Qushayri, al-Hujwiri

Al-Harith al-Muhasibi (d. 243/857) is the earliest systematic author on the examination of the
self: his *Ri'aya li-huquq Allah* treats *muhasaba* (accounting), *muraqaba* (watchfulness),
*riya'* (ostentation), *'ujb* (conceit) and the diagnosis of the *nafs*'s ruses; Picken 2011 is the
standard English study (life, works, and the concept of *tazkiyat al-nafs*). Al-Qushayri's *Risala*
(written 438/1046; Knysh 2007) contains a short lexicon of Sufi technical terms that defines
*nafs* (the blameworthy qualities of the servant), *ruh* (a subtle entity deposited in the body),
*sirr* (the innermost secret, the locus of witnessing), and *qalb*; it also has chapters on
*muraqaba* and *muhasaba*. Al-Hujwiri's *Kashf al-mahjub* (Nicholson 1911), the oldest Persian
treatise on Sufism, has sections on the nature of the *nafs* (the "animal soul, commanding to evil",
the greatest veil between God and man) and on the schools that disputed whether *ruh* is created.

## 8. Where scholars (and the tradition) disagree

1. **Are *nafs* and *ruh* the same thing?** Many theologians and hadith scholars (e.g. Ibn
   al-Qayyim in *Kitab al-Ruh*) treat them as one entity with two names; al-Ghazali says the four
   words name one subtlety in their second sense but keeps a physical *ruh* (vapour) and a
   blameworthy *nafs* (appetite + anger) as distinct first senses; Avicennan philosophers use *nafs*
   for the form/perfection of the body and *ruh* for the physicians' pneuma; Sufi lexica
   (al-Qushayri) distinguish *nafs*, *qalb*, *ruh*, *sirr* as successively subtler faculties or
   "levels" (*lata'if*). EI2 "Nafs"; Knysh 2007; Skellie 2010; Picken 2011.
2. **Is *'aql* a faculty, a body of knowledge, or a substance?** Kalam theologians define *'aql* as
   a set of necessary cognitions (an accident in the knower); the philosophers make the human
   intellect a faculty of the rational soul while the Active Intellect is a separate substance;
   al-Ghazali gives *'aql* both an "attribute" and a "subtlety" sense; Ibn 'Arabi treats *'aql* as a
   limited, "fettering" mode of knowing that the heart surpasses. Gutas 2014; Griffel 2009;
   Chittick 1989.
3. **Is the soul immaterial?** Avicenna: an immaterial substance from the start (flying man);
   early Ash'ari and Mu'tazili kalam and many hadith scholars: a subtle body (*jism latif*);
   al-Ghazali: publicly non-committal, privately (per Gianotti 2001 and Kukkonen 2012) close to
   Avicenna; Mulla Sadra: material in origin, immaterial in subsistence. Modern scholars also
   disagree about *what al-Ghazali himself believed* (Gianotti vs. Griffel vs. Treiger).
4. **Whether the Active Intellect is needed.** Avicenna's emanation of intelligibles from a separate
   intellect was criticised by al-Ghazali (as part of his critique of the philosophers' cosmology),
   reinterpreted by Suhrawardi in terms of light, and replaced by Mulla Sadra's existential union
   of knower and known. SEP "Ibn Sina", "Suhrawardi", "Mulla Sadra".
5. **How many stages of the nafs?** Three in the Qur'an; seven in most later manuals; the order and
   the name of the highest stage vary by order and author (sec. 1 above).
6. **The flying man: demonstration or pointer?** Marmura 1986 (pointer); Kaukua 2015 and Ivry (SEP)
   on its role in establishing the soul's substantiality and self-awareness.

## 9. Guidance for respectful analogical use in an engineering paper

Sensitivities. (a) *Ruh*: the Qur'an says "the spirit is of the command of my Lord, and you have
been given but little knowledge of it" (Q 17:85, قُلِ ٱلرُّوحُ مِنۡ أَمۡرِ رَبِّي); the tradition
treats it as deliberately beyond human analysis. (b) Attributing a soul, consciousness, *fitra*,
or moral accountability to software is theologically loaded and, for most Muslim readers, false.
(c) The Active Intellect and the heart's illumination are, in the sources, about God's
relation to the human being; mapping them onto a retrieval index or a reward model can read as
trivialising. (d) The *nafs* stages are stations of a believer's journey to God, not performance
tiers. (e) Hadith and verses are sacred text; paraphrase precisely and cite by number.

Five do/don't rules:

1. **DO** present every borrowing as an explicit *analogy* and say so once, early ("we borrow the
   vocabulary as a design metaphor; no claim about the metaphysics of the soul is intended").
   **DON'T** write "the system has a nafs/qalb/ruh"; write "the component we *call* the nafs
   layer, by analogy with al-Ghazali's usage".
2. **DO** use the Qur'anic/Ghazalian triad *nafs - 'aql - qalb* (impulse / deliberation /
   integrating executive) and the Avicennan *internal senses* (perception, estimation, memory,
   compositive imagination), which are faculty-psychology and therefore closest to an
   information-processing reading. **DON'T** model the *ruh* at all, and **don't** map the Active
   Intellect, divine *ilham*, or *mukashafa* to any software component; if you need "external
   source of knowledge", call it that.
3. **DO** keep the normative asymmetry of the sources: the "commanding" *nafs* is to be
   governed, not optimised; *muhasaba*/*muraqaba* are *self-correction under a standard*, which maps
   well to evaluation/monitoring loops. **DON'T** use the seven-stage ladder as a maturity model or
   benchmark scale, and don't invent new stages.
4. **DO** cite Qur'an by surah:verse with a named translation (Abdel Haleem 2008) and hadith by
   collection and number (Bukhari 52; Muslim 1599a); **DON'T** quote unsourced "hadith" (e.g. the
   "heart contains Me" saying, or "the first thing God created was the intellect", which is
   considered fabricated by hadith critics) and don't paraphrase scripture loosely.
5. **DO** acknowledge the disagreements in sec. 8 in a footnote (different orders, different
   authors), attribute each concept to a specific author and text, and have a Muslim scholar or
   reader review the final analogy section. **DON'T** present "the Islamic view of the soul" as a
   single fixed scheme, and don't put Qur'anic Arabic or the divine name in diagrams of system
   components.
