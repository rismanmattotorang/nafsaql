\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Setup and method}
\label{sec:eval:setup}
All measurements were taken on the reference machine of
Section~\ref{sec:inference}, which was shared with other workloads throughout
(load average typically 5 to 30 on four cores, at times above 100). Absolute
times are therefore pessimistic lower bounds on speed; token counts, verdicts
and rankings do not depend on load. Three rules were applied to every
comparison. (i)~Arms are \emph{interleaved} on the same items, with their
order rotated, so that a drift in load hits every arm; twice a sequential run
suggested a gain (30\,\% from a cache format, a recall gain from a graph arm)
that an interleaved or re-based repeat removed. (ii)~A change becomes a
default only on a measured gain; otherwise it stays off and is reported.
(iii)~Sample sizes are stated; they are far below those of
Proposition~\ref{prop:samples}, so quality differences are indicative.
Benchmarks are GSM8K \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k} for mathematics, MBPP \cite{austin2021mbpp} for code, a JSON-Schema task set, and
question sets over this paper's own sources for retrieval.

\textbf{Which confidence signal predicts a wrong draft?} 150 GSM8K items were
drafted by \Sone\ under production settings and scored by the exact verifier
(31 wrong). The best token signal is the single lowest token log-probability
(AUROC 0.646 [0.539, 0.746]); the mean log-probability, on which the fixed
rule rests, has 0.564 [0.449, 0.672], and at its production threshold it
escalates none of the 150 drafts, because mathematics drafts have a mean of
about $-0.03$ whether right or wrong. Agreement with two cheap samples is the
stronger signal: on 60 of the drafts (12 wrong) its AUROC is 0.759 [0.618,
0.901], and 0.823 with ties broken by the lowest token; the rule ``any
disagreement'' escalates 25\,\% of drafts and catches 8 of the 12 wrong ones
(precision 0.53), which raises the expected cascade accuracy from 0.800 to
0.908--0.933. This is why unverified mathematics drafts always get their
samples. The precision is still below the 0.65 that
Theorem~\ref{thm:gate} requires at the default price, so a promoted
calibration will price the signal rather than apply it unconditionally.

\textbf{Certified bins on a live ledger.} Probes on 204 GSM8K and MBPP
training items gave 203 labelled drafts, 51 of them wrong. The fit is well
calibrated (held-out ECE 0.024, Brier 0.117). Over 400 random 60/40 splits
and about 1{,}300 held-out bin tests, one bin broke its bound significantly,
so the certificate holds out of sample. At this size it costs coverage: on the
same held-out drafts the fixed rule keeps 78.2\,\% at 10.1\,\% wrong, the
calibrated rule on expected loss alone keeps 85.8\,\% at 16.6\,\% wrong, and
with certified bins it keeps 63.6\,\% at 9.5\,\% (risk 0.40), 54.5\,\% at
9.9\,\% (0.25) and 5.4\,\% at 12.4\,\% (0.15). Bounds from a hundred drafts
are too wide to beat a rule that uses the samples' agreement, so the fixed
rule stays in force until the label stream has filled the ledger. The stream
has since raised the ledger to 1{,}294 labelled drafts (280 wrong). A refit
on them is as well calibrated (held-out ECE 0.023 over five folds, Brier
0.115), and of its five equal bins one is certified under the 0.15 bound
(upper bound 0.106) and two sit just above it (0.157 and 0.161). The fit was
not promoted: the promotion rule asks for 2{,}900 held-out drafts, and the
system applies its own rule.

\textbf{Early escalation and the reasoning exit.} In an agreement study,
\EarlyEscSummary. With the adaptive reasoning exit, \AdaptiveExitSummary. On
six factual prompts the posterior exit rule gave the same content as the
two-agreeing-probes rule with slightly more reasoning (382 against 357 tokens
on average), so the simpler rule is the default.

The append-only transcript with a per-step grammar verified more tasks on
both models, and the mechanism is visible in every step: 23 of 50 steps of
the free-text loop held no action the harness could parse, against 1 of 41
under the grammar. The difference in verified tasks on \Sone\ rests on two
discordant tasks (exact McNemar $p=0.5$). Offering a patch at every step was
the worst arm: most patches applied and still broke the file, so the grammar
fixes the syntax of a patch, not its sense. A verified \Stwo\ fix of a small
function takes 33 to 59\,s with the cheap first step, where the free-text
loop spent single calls of 500 to 1{,}041\,s and verified none. The cascade
verified the same ten tasks in 26\,\% less wall time (faster in 7 of 10
pairs, sign test $p=0.17$); six tasks never reached \Stwo. The guards
(compile check, rollback, stall guard) cost nothing: with them on and off,
ten tasks on each model verified identically (10/10 on \Stwo; 4/10 and 5/10
in two rounds on \Sone), with the same number of model calls. They also
changed no outcome on this task set; they are insurance against failure
modes seen in practice, such as a run that spent its whole 900\,s cap on
repeated indentation errors. Pruning old observations before compaction is
likewise inert here: the model is never shown more than about 930 bytes of
evidence, and 16 paired runs had identical action sequences.

\textbf{Lean.} The protocol is validated end to end with the real binary, but
Qwen3-4B-Thinking in 4 bits does not yet write valid Lean~4: for $n+0=n$,
which \texttt{rfl} proves, it abstained after three attempts (826\,s). Every
verdict was precise and no wrong proof was accepted; the system abstained
with a notice.

\subsection{Does the knowledge base find and use the right passage?}
\label{sec:eval:knowledge}
\textbf{Answers.} Twenty questions with exact expected phrases were asked over
this paper's own sources in each mode, arms interleaved per question. On a
66-passage base with the model-built graph (271 entities, 100 relations, each
with a verified quote): closed-book 0/20, \texttt{rag} 18/20 (median 20.8\,s),
\texttt{graph} 17/20 (25.2\,s). On the 847-passage base: closed-book 0/20,
\texttt{rag} 17/20 ($p=1.5\times10^{-5}$ against closed-book). On an earlier
fixture base closed-book answered 2/20 against 18/20 for \texttt{rag}. Before
the graph became a fused arm it had answered 15/20 and lost all three
discordant pairs to \texttt{rag}, because it reordered passages by entity
mentions; as an arm it loses one pair and wins none. It no longer costs
accuracy, adds none on these questions and costs 15\,\% of the time, so the
automatic mode uses \texttt{rag}. Every miss is classified: two questions
were not retrieved, one was retrieved but not used.

\textbf{Is the answer in its sources?} The support check of
Section~\ref{sec:kb:chat} was measured on 50 questions with exact expected
phrases. The quick model answered each twice, from the passages (42 of 50
right) and with none (0 of 50), and the answers made up without passages
were checked against the passages the other answer had been given
(Table~\ref{tab:support}). A made-up answer is flagged 38 times in 44; a
right answer from passages once in 42; the area under the curve for telling
the two apart is 0.90. The check does not catch a wrong reading of the right
passage: of the five wrong answers from passages that make a claim, three
quote a number that is in a passage and is the wrong one. Two design points
came from this run. A word that the answer repeats from the question counts
as found (without it five right answers had a sentence flagged, such as
``listens on port 8300'' against a passage that writes \texttt{:8300});
requiring instead that every other word be in a passage flagged 16. And a
sentence that says the base does not hold the answer is set aside rather
than counted as an unsupported claim. Sending answers below a supported
share of 0.5 to the reasoning model would have moved two of the 50, both
wrong; two answers do not show that the reasoning model would then be right,
so that rule is off.

\subsection{Do memory tokens carry knowledge?}
\label{sec:eval:memory}
Not at this scale. The module was fine-tuned for one epoch on the 66-passage
base (234 examples; validation loss 8.77 to 1.46). On the 20 questions:
closed-book 0/20, \texttt{rag} 18/20, \texttt{hybrid} 17/20, \texttt{memory}
alone 0/20 with degenerate text. In the training decoder the claim accuracy is
0.55 with the module and 0.55 without it: the module read no knowledge, as its
authors predict for a module without pre-training. Training costs 137 to
272\,s per example here, and the pre-training stages need a GPU. An untrained
module reads exactly like no module, so the path itself is correct.

A second look at the trained module explains the result. Its forward pass,
recomputed outside the system and matched to the reference within
$9\times10^{-7}$, gives \emph{one vector for all sixteen slots and for every
question}: the cosine between slots is 0.9999999, between the memories of
different questions 0.999998, and the vector's norm is 63 where a token
embedding of the decoder has 1.5. The residual branches had drifted to large
constants and the projection was close to rank one. The module had become a
fixed prompt for the answer format, which lowers the loss and carries no
knowledge, and selecting the checkpoint by validation loss had rewarded it.
Against the reference, the first trainer used three times the learning rate
at a quarter of the batch, no dropout and a warm-up that started near the
peak. These are corrected and the collapse is now measured and refused
(Section~\ref{sec:mem:tokens}); a retrain with the corrected recipe was not
run, since it costs hours for one small base and its outcome without
pre-training is uncertain.

\subsection{Does the memory of earlier conversations work?}
\label{sec:eval:recall}
A live check of fourteen assertions passes. A fact kept on purpose
(``Remember that the staging server listens on port 8443'') and a fact stated
in passing in another conversation were each recalled from a new conversation
and used in the answer; a question in other words (``which pet does my
sibling own?'' for ``my sister's dog is called Biscuit'') found its note
through the meaning arm; an impersonal question and a private request read
nothing; the conversation that held the fact answered from its own history;
a forgotten note and the notes of a deleted conversation were not recalled;
and deleting the conversations left the memory as it had been. When the gate
opens, the memory step takes 0.13 to 0.58\,s under load 20 to 54, nearly all
of it one embedding call; when it does not, the step costs nothing. This is a
check of function, not of recall quality over a long history: no set of real
multi-session conversations with known answers was available.

\subsection{Validation}
\label{sec:eval:validation}
The workspace has 513 Rust tests, including the ported upstream suites of the
patch parser and the Lean rules, bit-identity tests of the batched engine and
parity tests of the memory module; formatting, lints and dependency audits
are clean. Live sweeps on the final build pass: the API contract, 26 of 26
security probes (sandbox escape, injection, authentication, limits) with no
open high-severity finding, 33 of 33 knowledge-security checks, 69 of 69
end-to-end checks of every endpoint and page, 10 of 10 chat-room checks, 55
knowledge checks, 14 of 14 checks of the conversation memory, 53 of 53 checks
of the agent harness and all nine acceptance tests (the first token of a
quick reply after 0.25\,s; a verified deliberate answer after 155\,s against
a target of 240\,s). The acceptance latencies depend on what else the machine
is doing: an earlier sweep the same day, under a load average of 30 to 40
from other workloads, missed both targets (2.6\,s and 420\,s) with correct
answers. The security suite earlier found one high-severity issue, since
fixed: sandboxed code could signal other processes.
