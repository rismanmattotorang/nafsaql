\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reasoning-tuned language models answer hard questions by spending more
computation at inference time: they sample long chains of thought, draw several
candidates, and verify or vote among them
\cite{wei2022cot,wang2023selfconsistency,snell2024scaling,deepseek2025r1}. The
gain is real but expensive, and it is wasted on the many requests that a direct
answer would have handled \cite{chen2024overthinking,zhang2025adaptthink}. This
is the engineering form of an old observation about cognition: there is a fast,
automatic mode and a slow, effortful one, and competence lies in knowing when to
switch \cite{kahneman2011thinking,evans2013dual,sloman1996two}.

\subsection{Problem}
Small models are fast and wrong too often. Large models are careful and need a
GPU or a remote service. Neither says which of its answers to trust. We ask what
can be built under a hard constraint: pretrained, openly licensed Qwen models of
at most 4B parameters, a machine with four CPU cores and no accelerator, and
nothing leaving that machine. Under this constraint a reasoning model decodes
5 to 8 tokens per second, so a deliberate answer takes minutes; the system must
spend that time only where it buys correctness, and must be able to say when it
did not get a checked answer.

\subsection{Inspiration}
The name joins two terms from the classical philosophy of mind of the Islamic
intellectual tradition: \emph{nafs}, the impulsive self that acts first, and
\emph{\textquoteleft aql}, the faculty of reason that restrains and corrects it.
We use the pair only as a naming analogy for a fast process and a deliberate
one, in the same spirit as the System~1 and System~2 of dual-process
psychology. Nothing in the design depends on the analogy, and every component
has a plain engineering name.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A formal model of a verifier-gated dual-process system with seventeen
stated results, each with explicit assumptions and the design decision it
fixes (Section~\ref{sec:math}).
\item An architecture in which acceptance needs evidence: an expected-loss gate
with certified per-bin risk, exact verifiers whose verdict is final, bounded
loops, and an assurance level on every reply
(Sections~\ref{sec:arch} and~\ref{sec:gate}).
\item A verifier-scored agent harness for small models: a best-first candidate
tree, an append-only transcript that keeps the engine's prompt cache valid, a
per-step decoding grammar that leaves the reasoning span free, and a cascade
that tries the quick model first (Section~\ref{sec:harness}).
\item A CPU inference layer, including an engine that serves several users in
one pass over the weights with output identical to a solo run, and a measured
account of which forms of parallelism pay on four cores
(Section~\ref{sec:inference}).
\item A knowledge base that searches documents by words and meaning from an
in-process index, builds a graph of a document set without a model call,
answers with numbered sources and says which sentences of an answer its
sources hold; and two memories that need no model, one of verified answers
and one of earlier conversations (Sections~\ref{sec:knowledge}
and~\ref{sec:memory}).
\item Measurements of all of the above on one shared machine, with interleaved
comparisons and the negative results reported beside the positive ones
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}), and a comparison with related open systems
(Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What this paper does not claim}
The quality results are indicative: comparisons use tens to a few hundred
items, far below what a release decision needs, and every time was measured on
a machine shared with other workloads. No public retrieval benchmark was run,
so the knowledge results are measured changes against our own earlier versions,
not against published systems. Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists what remains open.
